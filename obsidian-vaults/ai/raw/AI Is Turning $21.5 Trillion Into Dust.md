Everyone is talking about AI like it’s a technology story.

It’s not.

## It’s an economics story.

The largest involuntary wealth transfer in modern history, happening in real-time, with quarterly earnings reports as the receipt. And the numbers aren’t projections from some think-tank PDF nobody reads. They’re measurements. From the Bureau of Labor Statistics. From the Federal Reserve. From the people whose literal job is counting money.

> In Q3 2025, U.S. workers took home 53.8% of GDP. That’s the **lowest** **share** since the BLS started recording this data in 1947.

Read that number again. Not a forecast. Not a model. A measurement. In 1980, that number was 58%. ==Six percentage points== of the entire U.S. economy shifted from the people who do the work to the people who own the machines that replaced the work.

Are you awake now? Read up. This is critical for you because there is still some time.

## The Magnificent Seven Ate Your Economy

Let’s start with where the money actually went.

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/format:webp/0*4ChXPid87hLMBC2U.jpg)

The Magnificent Seven (Apple, Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, Nvidia, Tesla) had a combined market capitalization of $21.5 trillion as of November 2025. That’s 37% of the S&P 500’s total market value. In 2015, that number was 12%. In ten years, seven companies went from owning one-eighth of America’s stock market to owning more than one-third.

### Not gradually. Exponentially.

==Nvidia alone surged 800% since January 2023==. OpenAI’s valuation hit $852 billion in March 2026, up from $300 billion twelve months earlier. That’s a nearly 3x increase in one year for a company that, by its own CEO’s admission, is still figuring out its business model.

==AI-related names drove 60% of the S&P 500’s value increase in 2025.== Strip out technology and communication services, and the S&P 500 returned just 6% for the year. The “market” isn’t going up. Seven companies are going up. Everything else is treading water.

## Where the Capital Expenditure Is Flowing

The Magnificent Seven’s ==collective capital expenditure surged from roughly 10% to 30% of total S&P 500 capex in just six years==. Alphabet, Microsoft, Meta, and Amazon are expected to spend nearly $700 billion combined in 2026 on AI infrastructure. Google alone committed $75 billion in 2025. Meta plans $65 billion against $185 billion in revenues; that’s 35% of sales going straight into data centers.

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/format:webp/0*ttp-n-lOhcO14UEx.png)

[https://www.glenmedeim.com/news-and-insights/market-snapshot-end-of-the-magnificent-7s-asset-light-era/](https://www.glenmedeim.com/news-and-insights/market-snapshot-end-of-the-magnificent-7s-asset-light-era/)

To put this in perspective: AI spending hit an estimated $427 billion in 2025 and is projected at $562 billion in 2026. And AI investment accounted for 92% of U.S. GDP growth in the first half of 2025.

Read that again. Ninety-two percent.

But wait. Information-processing equipment was only 4% of GDP. Without data center capital expenditure, GDP growth would have been 0.1% in H1 2025. The economy isn’t growing because of productivity. It’s growing because a handful of companies are writing checks for servers. Remove those checks, and you’re looking at a flatline.

## 56% of CEOs Got Nothing They’re Still Firing.

### Now here’s where it gets ugly.

PwC’s 2026 Global CEO Survey covered 4,454 CEOs across 95 countries. The headline finding: 56% say they’ve gotten “nothing out of” their AI investments. Only 12% report AI both grew revenues AND reduced costs. Nine out of ten companies can’t point to a single productivity metric that moved.

### MIT’s 2025 study was worse

95% of enterprise AI pilot programs are failing to deliver measurable financial returns. Gartner found only 28% of AI use cases fully succeed and meet ROI expectations; 20% fail outright.

==Robert Solow wrote in 1987==

> “You can see the computer age everywhere but in the productivity statistics.” He was talking about computers. Thirty-nine years later, we’re running the same experiment with AI, at 100x the capital intensity, and getting the same non-result.

Are we serious?

## The ROI Bifurcation Nobody Admits

Here’s the thing: there IS an ROI story. It’s just not the one being sold.

Top-performing companies report $10.30 in returns per dollar invested. The average company? The median AI ROI timeline is 2–4 years, compared to 7–12 months for typical tech investments. Only 6% reported payback in under one year. The ROI curve is a cliff: a tiny elite prints money; everyone else burns it.

### This isn’t a technology adoption curve

It’s a winner-take-all dynamic masquerading as a technology adoption curve. The top 5% of firms capture the value. The other 95% subsidize the infrastructure buildout through their failed pilots, talent competition, and cloud computing bills that flow straight to… the Magnificent Seven.

So. ==The money flows up.== The productivity doesn’t flow anywhere. And the firing happens regardless.

Because here’s the resolution to the apparent paradox: companies aren’t automating because AI is productive. They’re automating because bad AI is cheaper than humans. A chatbot that handles 70% of customer inquiries poorly is still cheaper than 4,000 customer service agents. Block proved this in March 2026 when it cut from 10,000 to fewer than 6,000 employees, the largest single workforce reduction explicitly attributed to AI automation. Their AI systems resolved 70–80% of inquiries without human intervention.

### Did service quality improve?

Nobody asked. Costs went down. That was the point…

Truth be told, this is the worst possible combination: no productivity miracle AND massive job displacement. The gains that historically offset displacement (cheaper goods, new industries, higher output) don’t materialize. But the displacement happens anyway. Because the “savings” aren’t productivity gains; they’re wealth transfers from labor to capital.

## The Job Destruction Ledger

Let me be specific. Not projections. Measurements.

In 2025, Challenger, Gray & Christmas tracked nearly 55,000 U.S. job cuts directly attributed to AI. The full year saw 1.17 million total job cuts, a 54% increase from 2024. In the first four months of 2026: 78,557 tech workers laid off, with 47.9% attributed to AI and automation.

Goldman Sachs puts the ongoing damage at 16,000 net jobs erased per month. That’s 25,000 jobs displaced by AI substitution, with 9,000 added back through augmentation. The net: a slow, steady bleed.

### Who’s Getting Hit

Not who you’d expect. Not factory workers. Not truck drivers. Not yet.

Legal services: document review, contract drafting, due diligence. Junior associates and paralegals are the most exposed. Finance: January 2025 saw the lowest professional services job openings since 2013, a 20% year-over-year drop. Software development: fresh graduate hiring by Big Tech declined 50% over three years. Customer service: 80% displacement risk; Block’s case study is the template.

Bloomberg’s analysis found AI could replace 53% of market research analyst tasks and 67% of sales representative tasks. Managerial roles? Only 9–21% automation risk.

See the pattern?

The white-collar middle is getting hollowed from below. Entry-level and mid-level cognitive work is being automated. Senior roles that require judgment, relationships, and political navigation remain. The career ladder isn’t shrinking. The bottom rungs are disappearing.

## The Generational Fracture

This is where the data gets genuinely alarming.

Entry-level job postings dropped 29% since January 2024. Junior tech positions in the U.S. declined 67%. Recent college graduate underemployment hit 42.5% in Q4 2025. Unemployment among 20-to-30-year-olds in AI-exposed occupations rose nearly 3 percentage points since early 2025.

> ==Anthropic’s CEO warned that AI could eliminate roughly 50% of entry-level white-collar positions within five years==. Cornell research found U.S. companies adopting AI reduced junior hiring by 13%. And 77% of new AI jobs require master’s degrees.

So the jobs being destroyed are entry-level. The jobs being created require advanced degrees. If you’re 23 with a bachelor’s degree and $40,000 in student debt, the math doesn’t work.

Goldman Sachs found that displaced workers face a decade of lower wages, with 10–30% pay cuts in new roles and longer job searches. They call it the “scarring effect.” I’d call it something less clinical.

## Who Owns the Gains? (You Already Know)

Now. Let’s connect the capital expenditure to the ownership.

### Federal Reserve data from Q2 2025

The top 1% owns $25.6 trillion in corporate stock and mutual fund shares. That’s exactly half of the $51.2 trillion total. The top 20% owns 93% of all stock.

So when Nvidia surges 800%, who benefits? When the Magnificent Seven drive 60% of market gains, who captures that? When AI-related companies account for an estimated 80% of U.S. stock market gains by October 2025, whose net worth goes up?

Not the 42.5% of recent college graduates who are underemployed. Not the 68% of Americans living paycheck to paycheck. Not the workers whose real wages declined over five years because their 16.3% pay increase couldn’t keep pace with 22.6% cumulative inflation.

Billionaire wealth jumped 16% in 2025, three times faster than the five-year average, reaching $18.3 trillion (Oxfam, January 2026). Eight of the top ten AI companies are billionaire-controlled. Three companies command nearly 90% of the generative AI chatbot market.

### Venture capital tells the same story at the funding layer.

AI and ML investments represented 52.7% of total global venture capital deal value by end of 2025. That’s $211 billion, up 85% year-over-year. The second-largest sector, healthcare and biotech, got $71.7 billion. Sixty-six percent less. And 58% of AI funding was in megarounds of $500 million or more; ==a handful of mega-deals drive the entire investment landscape==.

The money isn’t “going into AI.” The money is going into seven companies and their satellite ecosystem, funded by venture capital that increasingly ignores everything else.

## Piketty Was Right. AI Is the Proof.

Thomas Piketty’s central thesis in *Capital in the Twenty-First Century* was deceptively simple: when the rate of return on capital (r) exceeds the rate of economic growth (g), wealth concentrates. r > g. ==Over time, the rich get richer not because they work harder but because capital compounds faster than the economy grows.==

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/format:webp/0*Y7hXZfr4dFhJQFVo.jpg)

### AI is r > g on steroids.

The S&P 500 returned roughly 47% since January 2023. Median wage growth over the same period: 3.8%. Stock ownership concentrated in the top 20% (93% of all shares). Wage income spread across the bottom 80%.

Capital returned 12x more than wages.

And ownership of that capital is concentrated in the hands of the few.

The IMF’s April 2025 working paper (“AI Adoption and Inequality,” WP/25/68) made this explicit: “Wealth inequality always increases with AI adoption because AI leads to labor displacement and increased demand for AI capital, boosting capital returns and asset holdings’ value.” ==Always.== Not “sometimes.” Not “in certain conditions.” Always.

## How the Gains Get Captured

Here’s the mechanism. An EY survey (December 2025) found that companies reinvest AI-driven productivity gains primarily into: expanding existing AI capabilities (47%), developing new AI capabilities (42%), and R&D (39%). Only 24% return capital to stakeholders. Only 17% reduce headcount explicitly; instead, they freeze hiring and let attrition do the work quietly.

> Translation: AI profits go into more AI. The flywheel spins. ==Capital compounds.== Labor watches.

CEO compensation in 2024: median $17.1 million in the S&P 500, a 9.7% increase. Median employee compensation: $85,419, a 1.7% increase. CEO pay grew 5.7 times faster than worker pay. And worker pay growth (16.3% over five years) trailed cumulative inflation (22.6%), meaning real wages actually declined.

At America’s 100 largest low-wage employers, CEOs earn 632 times the average worker. Not a typo. Six hundred and thirty-two.

When the preference is to lose lose than to win yourself.! Truly a self-destructive mechanism.

## The Energy Tax Nobody Mentions

But there is always a but.

The wealth transfer has a physical cost. And it’s a cost that acts as a regressive tax on everyone, including people who never touch AI.

==Global data center electricity consumption==: 415 terawatt-hours in 2024, projected to hit 1,100 TWh by 2026.

> A terawatt-hour is a billion kilowatt-hours; your house uses about 10,000 kWh per year. So 1,100 TWh powers roughly 110 million homes.

That’s equivalent to Japan’s entire national electricity consumption. For AI. In the PJM grid (mid-Atlantic, stretching from Illinois to North Carolina), electricity rates increased up to 20% in summer 2025 because of data center demand. Average residential bills rose $18 per month in western Maryland and $16 per month in Ohio.

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/format:webp/0*8fuein_AUpGpxuAx.jpg)

Data centers accounted for a $9.3 billion price increase in the 2025–26 PJM capacity market. Google alone spent $75 billion on AI infrastructure in 2025. U.S. cloud providers are projected to invest $600 billion in AI infrastructure in 2026.

By 2030, data center carbon emissions will reach 24–44 million metric tons annually.

In practical terms: that’s the annual exhaust of 5–10 million additional cars on the road, roughly the entire vehicle fleet of a state like Georgia. Water consumption: 450 million gallons per day. Picture filling 680 Olympic swimming pools. Every single day. That’s the daily water use of about 5 million people, ==being consumed to cool servers.==

Sorry, but this is a f\*\*k you to the people paying those electricity bills. A retiree in Ohio whose power bill went up $16 a month is subsidizing Google’s AI infrastructure. She doesn’t own Google stock. She doesn’t use AI. She’s paying anyway.

> AI’s energy cost is a regressive tax. The wealthy own the companies consuming the power. The poor pay the same per-kilowatt increase.

## The Valuation Question Hanging Over Everything

One more thing before I leave you with a verdict.

### The Shiller P/E ratio hit 38.2–39.35 as of March 2026

This is the cyclically adjusted price-to-earnings ratio: how much investors pay per dollar of inflation-adjusted earnings, averaged over ten years. Think of it as “how expensive is the stock market relative to what companies actually earn.” The higher the number, the more investors are paying for future hope rather than present reality.

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/format:webp/0*iIiAf3w_2bp9FsZx.jpg)

The historical mean is 17.34. Current: 38.2. That’s the second-highest in 155 years of market data, ==trailing only the dot-com peak of 44.19== in December 1999. Current valuations sit 135% above the long-run average.

### AI sector revenue multiples average 23.4x

Translation: investors are paying $23.40 for every $1 of current revenue. Anthropic sits at 27x ($380 billion valuation on $14 billion annualized revenue). Early-stage AI startups routinely exceed 70x, meaning investors pay $70 for every dollar the company brings in. Traditional SaaS companies trade at 5–10x. The gap is faith, not fundamentals.

Now. Here’s the nuance that separates this from 2000. Today’s mega-cap AI companies have real earnings, massive free cash flow, and proven revenue bases. The dot-com era was sock puppets and vaporware. This is different.

But. Implied 10-year annual returns at current valuations: 2.3%. Historical average: 7–10%. The market is pricing in perfection. And 56% of CEOs just told PwC they got nothing out of their AI investments.

It worked as a prototype; then all went down!

Or maybe it works this time. But the valuation premium assumes universal adoption, universal productivity gains, and universal ROI that 95% of pilots are currently failing to deliver. The base case for returns is mediocre even if everything goes right.

## Let me bring this home.

### This Is a Wealth Transfer, Not a Technology Revolution

The standard narrative goes: AI is a revolutionary technology that will increase productivity, create new industries, and ultimately benefit everyone, just like electricity, just like the internet. Growing pains are temporary. The market will adjust.

The data says otherwise.

AI investment: 92% of GDP growth. AI productivity gains: ==unmeasurable for 56% of adopters==. AI job displacement: 16,000 net per month. AI wealth concentration: top 1% owns 50% of stock. AI energy costs: regressive tax on everyone.

> The productivity benefits are theoretical. The wealth transfer is measured and real and ongoing.

Piketty warned that r > g would concentrate wealth unless policy intervened. AI made r > g the default economic operating system. Capital returns are compounding at 12x the rate of wage growth, ownership is concentrated in the top quintile, and the infrastructure costs are being socialized while the profits are being privatized.

This isn’t the usual AI hype cycle analysis. This isn’t about whether GPT-5 will pass the bar exam or whether agents will replace your coworkers. This is about who owns the economic output of a civilization that’s rapidly automating the work that 70% of its people do.

The answer, as of Q3 2025, is: not the people doing the work.

This is my perspective. You should do what you are comfortable with.

But the BLS numbers don’t have a perspective. They’re just numbers. And they say 53.8%. Lowest ever. While the Magnificent Seven hit $21.5 trillion.
